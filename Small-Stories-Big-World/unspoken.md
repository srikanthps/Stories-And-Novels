##🌅 The bridge of letters 🌅**


The afternoon sun slanted across the balcony of a high-rise apartment in Jayanagar, Bengaluru. Sixty-eight-year-old Madhav Rao sat with a sleek paperback on his lap. He traced his thumb over the Kannada title: **ಕಾರವಾಲೋ** (*Karvalo*). He smiled, a deep, triumphant warmth spreading through his chest as he read the full word without stumbling.

Madhav was born to a Kannada-speaking family, but his father’s banking job had swept them to New Delhi when he was just a toddler. In the vast northern plains, Kannada was reduced to a language spoken strictly within the four walls of their kitchen. He grew up writing impeccable English at school, speaking fluent Hindi with friends, and communicating in a functional, heavily broken mother tongue with his parents. To him, Kannada had just been a domestic tool—meant for asking what was for dinner or wishing an elder on the phone.

Two years ago, after retiring, Madhav moved back to Bengaluru. He wanted to reconnect with his roots, but he quickly felt like a leftover ghost in his own homeland. His accent was stiff, his vocabulary painfully basic, and his tongue tripped over the complex curves of the alphabet.

Frustrated by his own limitations, Madhav did something his tech-savvy grandkids thought was eccentric: at sixty-six, he bought a primary school textbook and began learning the script from scratch.

As the months bled into years, those looping characters began to untie themselves. He graduated from children's stories to the works of Poornachandra Tejaswi and K. Shivaram Karanth. That was when the true revelation hit him. Kannada wasn't just a medium of speech; it was an ocean of staggering richness. He discovered words that beautifully captured the exact smell of the first rain on dry soil, words for complex human emotions that English completely failed to describe, and philosophies that shook him to his core.

But with this newfound awe came a deep, aching sorrow.

Every evening, Madhav watched his son and daughter-in-law return from their corporate tech jobs. He listened to his teenage grandchildren talk. Their conversations were a jarring, hollow cocktail of English with a few Kannada verbs thrown in as an afterthought—an urban "Kanglish" spoken with absolute indifference.

*"Maga, tomorrow college ge leave ide, let's go out somewhere,"* his grandson would shout into a gaming headset.

It wasn't just his family. In the metro, in the sprawling malls, and in upscale cafes, he saw an entire generation completely detached from the weight of their own language. Urban families seemed absolutely happy, almost proud, that their children couldn't read the script or speak a single sentence of pure, classic Kannada. The language was being systematically pushed out of modern living rooms, relegated to street signs and domestic chores.

Madhav closed his book and looked out at the city skyline. A quiet tear of regret, but also of fierce determination, welled in his eyes. He felt deeply sorry for a society that was trading its ancient, golden heritage for a superficial convenience. They were starving themselves of their own literature, thinking they were being progressive.

"Thatha?"

Madhav looked up. His twelve-year-old granddaughter, Ananya, was standing at the balcony door, holding a smartphone. She glanced at the script on the book cover with total unfamiliarity. "What are you reading?"

Madhav looked at the book, then at her innocent face. The language wasn't dying because the kids hated it; it was dying because no one was introducing them to its beauty.

He opened the book to the very first page and tapped the wicker chair next to him.

"Come here, *kanda*," Madhav said, his voice soft but steady, speaking in the cleanest, warmest Kannada he could muster. "Sit with me. Let me tell you a story about a brilliant old scientist, a magical forest, and a flying lizard."

Ananya hesitated for a second, then smiled and sat down beside him. She leaned in, her eyes shifting from her smartphone screen to the open pages. The circular, looping shapes of the script looked to her like an intricate code. To a twelve-year-old raised on a strict diet of international school boards and internet streaming platforms, this was an unfamiliar world existing right inside her own living room.

"Thatha," she whispered, pointing to a word at the top of the page. "What is that?"

Madhav placed his wrinkled finger just beneath the letters. "**ಪರಿಸರ** (*Parisara*)," he said, letting the syllables roll out with deliberate, rhythmic clarity. "It means nature. But not just trees and plants, Ananya. It means everything that embraces you—the air, the soil, the creatures, and the invisible patterns connecting them all together."

"In English, we just say 'environment,'" Ananya muttered, tracking the word with her own small finger. "It sounds so... like a geography textbook topic. *Parisara* sounds softer."

"Exactly," Madhav said, his heart skipping a beat with a sudden rush of hope. "That is the secret of your mother tongue. English classifies the world. Kannada feels it."

For the next hour, the balcony became a time machine. Madhav didn't just read the story; he broke down the architecture of the sentences. He showed her how a single, well-placed *vattakshara* could alter the emotional weight of a line. He introduced her to Mandanna, the innocent village cowboy who could read the behavior of wild bees like an open book, and Professor Karvalo, the high-flying scientist who respected the uneducated cowboy's instinct more than any Western textbook.

As he spoke, he noticed something beautiful. The stiff, awkward barrier in his own speech began to melt away. For two years, he had been terrified of sounding foolish to native residents. But here, teaching his granddaughter, the purity of the language flowed out naturally, unshackled by the fear of judgment. He wasn't just reconstructing a lost heritage for her; he was actively healing his own lifelong sense of displacement.

Suddenly, the balcony door slid open further. Madhav’s son, Arvind, stood there holding a cup of filter coffee. He had his wireless office headset resting around his neck, a visual reminder of the fast-paced corporate world downstairs. He paused, looking at his father and daughter huddled over the Kannada paperback. He hadn't seen Ananya put her phone down voluntarily in months.

"What's going on here?" Arvind asked, his tone a mix of curiosity and slight amusement. "Exam preparation?"

"No, Papa," Ananya looked up, her face bright. "Thatha is showing me how *Karvalo* tracks a flying lizard. And look—" She pointed proudly to a blank notepad Madhav had used to practice his letters. "I just learned how to write my own name in Kannada. It's way more beautiful than the English spelling. See the curves?"

Arvind walked over, took a sip of his coffee, and looked down at the paper. For a fleeting second, a shadow of profound guilt crossed his face. He looked at his father—a man who had spent his entire adulthood in Delhi, who had fought tooth and nail to reclaim his language at an age when most people give up on learning—and then looked at his daughter, who had lived her entire life in the heart of Bengaluru but couldn't read a street sign in her own state.

Arvind sat down on the edge of the cane stool nearby. He reached out and touched the cover of *Karvalo*.

"I remember reading this in college," Arvind said softly, his voice dropping its usual corporate clip. "Before the software boom, before the long commutes... we used to debate Tejaswi’s books for hours at the local canteen."

"Then why did you stop speaking it cleanly at home, Arvind?" Madhav asked gently, completely devoid of anger, asking purely out of a grandfather's curiosity.

Arvind sighed, looking out at the sprawling concrete grid of Jayanagar. "I don't know, Pa. The world just became so fast. In the office, it's English. In the presentations, it's English. When we come home, we are so exhausted that we just slide into whatever language requires the least amount of mental resistance. Kanglish became the default. We didn't do it on purpose. It just... happened."

"That is how a language quietly passes away, my son," Madhav said, closing the book gently but keeping his hand firmly on it. "Not with a loud protest, but with a comfortable, silent shrug. You think you are just mixing words for convenience, but you are slowly erasing the vocabulary of your ancestors' emotions. If Ananya only knows how to say *'leave ide'*, she will never understand the true rest and peace of *'vishranthi'*."

The balcony fell into a profound, reflective silence. The distant hum of Bangalore's evening traffic buzzed below, a symbol of a city rushing blindly into a hyper-globalized future. But right there, on the third floor, a tiny island of resistance had formed.

Arvind looked at his father, then at his daughter. He took off his office headset and placed it on the table.

"Hey, Ananya," Arvind said, a small, genuine smile returning to his face. "Move over a bit. Let me see if I can still read that first chapter out loud without making a mistake. Thatha can grade both of us."

Ananya giggled and shifted closer to her grandfather. Madhav opened the book once more, the pages catching the warm gold of the setting sun. As Arvind began to read the first lines of classic Kannada, his voice shaky at first but growing stronger with every sentence, Madhav leaned back.

He realized that his years of struggle hadn't just been an old man's eccentric hobby. He had built a bridge. And for the first time since he moved back to Bengaluru, Madhav Rao felt like he was finally, truly, back home.

---

The next morning, Monday arrived with its usual chaotic rush. The apartment buzzed with the sounds of a typical urban household—the beep of the microwave, the chime of laptop notifications, and the rustle of ironed school uniforms.

Ananya stood by the dining table, strapping on her smart watch, her heavy school bag slung over one shoulder. She attended an elite, international-board school on Sarjapur Road, a place where the hallways echoed exclusively with English, accented with American slang picked up from internet streaming platforms.

Madhav sat at the table, sipping his tea, watching her. He reached into his pocket and pulled out a small, worn pocket notebook with a black cover. He placed it next to her tiffin box.

"What's this, Thatha?" Ananya asked, picking it up.

"Your secret weapon," Madhav said with a wink. "Inside, I have written down ten beautiful, classic Kannada words that you won't find in your school textbooks. Words for things you see every day. Take it with you."

Ananya smiled, slipped the little black notebook into her blazer pocket, and waved goodbye.

By lunchtime, the atmosphere at her school was loud and lively. Ananya sat around a sleek cafeteria table with her inner circle of friends—Meera, Rohan, and Kabir. They were debating a popular new sci-fi movie.

"The CGI was insane," Rohan said, gesturing wildly with a french fry. "But the ending was so... I don't know, it felt so empty. There isn’t a proper English word for that specific kind of hollow feeling you get when a good story just ends badly."

Ananya paused, her fork halfway to her mouth. Suddenly, she felt a strange, electric spark of memory. She reached into her pocket, pulled out the little black notebook, and flipped through her grandfather’s neat, handwritten pages.

"Actually, there is a word," Ananya said, her voice cutting through the cafeteria noise.

Her friends stopped talking, looking at her with raised eyebrows. Kabir laughed, "What language? French?"

"No," Ananya said proudly, looking directly at them. "Kannada. The phrase is ಬೆರಗು (Beragu) mixed with ಶೂನ್ಯತೆ (Shoonyate). My grandfather taught me yesterday. Beragu means a state of utter, breathless wonder or being deeply struck with awe. And Shoonyate isn't just being empty or bored; it’s that deep, heavy, existential stillness you feel when something grand suddenly disappears. Together, they mean you are left completely frozen in awe, staring into nothingness."

The table went completely quiet. Rohan blinked, stunned by the sheer accuracy of the definition.

Meera looked at the notebook in Ananya's hand, her expression a mix of confusion and surprise. "Wait, you speak Kannada? Like, real Kannada? I thought your family just used it to talk to the maid or the delivery guys."

"Yeah, Ananya," Kabir chimed in, chuckling nervously. "Why are you acting like an old-school literary professor suddenly? It’s just Kannada. Nobody our age talks like that. It's so uncool."

The word *uncool* hung in the air like a foul odor. A week ago, that single comment would have made Ananya shrink back, hide her notebook, and conform to the group. But today, looking at the intricate script her grandfather had stayed up late into the night writing for her, she felt a sudden surge of fierce, protective pride.

"Why is it uncool, Kabir?" Ananya asked, her voice calm but remarkably steady, mirroring the dignity she had seen in her grandfather’s eyes. "Because it belongs to the soil we are standing on right now? We learn French and Spanish words because we think it makes us look global, but we are absolutely blind to the wealth in our own backyard. That’s not being cool. That’s just being ignorant."

Meera looked down at her lap, a sudden flush of embarrassment coloring her cheeks. Rohan looked at the notebook with genuine curiosity. "Can I see that?" he asked softly.

Ananya slid the notebook across the table. Rohan flipped the pages, staring at the looping characters. "My grandparents live in Malleshwaram," he murmured, almost to himself. "They read these huge Kannada novels every afternoon. I always thought it was just an old people thing. I didn't realize the words actually meant something so deep."

"They do," Ananya said, leaning forward. "My Thatha says that when we stop using these words, we lose the ability to feel those exact emotions. We are literally shrinking our minds just to fit in."

Meanwhile, back in Jayanagar, Madhav Rao was having his own battle.

He was sitting on a stone bench at the local neighborhood park, a paperback copy of *Jugari Cross* open in his hands. A wealthy, retired neighbor named Murthy, dressed in a crisp track suit and expensive walking shoes, strolled over and sat beside him.

Murthy glanced at the Kannada book in Madhav’s hands and chuckled condescendingly. "Ah, Madhav. Still wasting your retirement years trying to decipher the local script? Honestly, why bother? Everything important in the world—business, science, global politics—happens in English. Why stress your brain with regional languages at this age?"

Madhav placed a dried leaf as a bookmark and closed the book gently. He looked at Murthy, a man who had lived in Bengaluru his whole life but prided himself on his children living in the US and speaking only English.

"Murthy avare," Madhav said, his voice smooth and untroubled. "I spent forty years in Delhi writing English reports for the government. It filled my bank account, yes. But it never filled my soul. A language is not just a commercial tool to make money. It is a lens through which you view existence."

He stood up, holding the book close to his chest. "When I read Tejaswi describing the mist rolling over the Western Ghats, I am not just reading words. I am breathing the history of my ancestors. Your English can explain the mechanics of a cloud, but it cannot capture the sacred relationship between a Kannada farmer and his rain. You think you are being practical, but you are leaving your grandchildren spiritually homeless in their own city."

Murthy opened his mouth to argue, but the sheer conviction in Madhav’s eyes silenced him. He watched, speechless, as the old man walked away with the posture of a king.

That evening, the family gathered around the dining table. Arvind was typing furiously on his laptop, still caught in the corporate web. Ananya walked in, pulled out her chair, and placed the little black notebook back in front of her grandfather.

"How did it go, *kanda*?" Madhav asked quietly.

"Rohan wants to learn three words tomorrow, Thatha," Ananya said, a brilliant, triumphant smile lighting up her face. "And Meera asked if she could borrow *Karvalo* once we are done reading it."

Arvind stopped typing. His hands hovered over the keyboard. He looked at his daughter, noticing a new kind of confidence in her—a grounded, self-assured maturity that her expensive school curriculum had never been able to teach her. Slowly, deliberately, Arvind closed his laptop screen shut.

"Alright," Arvind said, looking at his father with deep reverence. "No more office work at the dinner table. Ma, Pa, Ananya... let's talk. And tonight, let's see who can go the whole dinner speaking only the most beautiful, pure Kannada words we know."

Madhav looked around the table. The sorrow that had weighed down his heart for two years completely evaporated, replaced by a radiant, timeless joy. The city outside was still loud, fast, and indifferent—but inside this room, the roots had finally caught hold of the soil.
